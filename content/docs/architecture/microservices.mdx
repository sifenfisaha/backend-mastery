## Why this matters

Microservices are the most over-prescribed medicine in backend engineering. Split a system into
independently deployable services and you *can* get faster teams, isolated failures, and
targeted scaling. Get the boundaries wrong and you've bought yourself a distributed monolith:
all the network latency, partial failures, and operational overhead of a distributed system with
none of the independence. A senior engineer isn't the one who reaches for microservices — it's
the one who can explain *when the split pays for itself* and, just as often, argues to stay a
monolith. This topic is about earning the right to distribute.

<MentorNote>
Don't confuse "microservices" with "scalability." A well-built monolith scales horizontally just
fine — run more copies behind a load balancer. Microservices don't buy you throughput; they buy
you **independent deployability** and **team autonomy**. If you can't name the team boundary or
release cadence a split unlocks, you're paying the distributed-systems tax for nothing.
</MentorNote>

## Mental model

Think of a company org chart:

- A **monolith** is one big open-plan room — everyone shares the same codebase, database, and
  deploy. Fast to build, easy to reason about, but everyone steps on each other as it grows.
- A **microservice** is a small autonomous team with its own office, its own filing cabinet
  (database), and its own door to the outside (API). It ships on its own schedule.
- The **service boundary** is where one team's responsibility ends and another's begins — draw it
  along business capabilities (Orders, Payments, Inventory), not technical layers.
- **Communication** between teams is either a phone call (synchronous — you wait for the answer)
  or a memo dropped in an inbox (asynchronous — fire and forget, they process it later).

Conway's Law is the punchline: your architecture will end up mirroring your org chart whether you
plan for it or not. Design the boundaries deliberately, or your reporting structure will design
them for you.

## Core concepts to master

<Accordions type="single">
<Accordion title="Monolith first — and the honest trade-offs">
Martin Fowler's advice is blunt: **almost all successful microservice stories started as a
monolith that got too big**. A monolith gives you one deploy, one database, in-process calls (no
network), and trivial transactions. You trade that for independent deployability and team
autonomy only when the monolith's coupling actually hurts. Split too early and you're guessing at
boundaries you don't understand yet — and boundaries are expensive to move once services own
their data.
</Accordion>
<Accordion title="Boundaries via DDD & bounded contexts">
The hardest part isn't the plumbing — it's *where to cut*. Use Domain-Driven Design: a **bounded
context** is a slice of the business with its own consistent model and language ("customer" in
Billing means something different than in Support). Align each service to one bounded context and
one business capability. Boundaries drawn along business domains stay stable; boundaries drawn
along technical layers (a "database service", a "logic service") create chatty, coupled messes.
</Accordion>
<Accordion title="Communication: sync (REST/gRPC) vs async (events)">
**Synchronous** (REST over HTTP, or gRPC for low-latency internal calls): the caller blocks and
waits. Simple to reason about, but it couples availability — if the callee is down, you're down,
and long call chains multiply latency. **Asynchronous** (publish events to a broker): the sender
doesn't wait, services stay decoupled and resilient, but you inherit eventual consistency and
harder debugging. Prefer async events for cross-service workflows; use sync only when you truly
need an immediate answer.
</Accordion>
<Accordion title="Data ownership: database-per-service">
The defining rule of microservices: **each service owns its own database, and no other service
touches it directly**. Want another service's data? Ask via its API or subscribe to its events.
The **shared-database anti-pattern** — multiple services reading and writing the same tables —
feels convenient but recouples everything: you can't change a schema without coordinating deploys,
and you've rebuilt the monolith with extra network hops. Own your data or don't split.
</Accordion>
<Accordion title="API gateway & service discovery">
Clients shouldn't know about 30 internal services. An **API gateway** is the single front door:
it routes requests, handles auth, rate limiting, and TLS termination, and can aggregate calls.
Internally, services find each other via **service discovery** (a registry like Consul, or
platform DNS in Kubernetes) instead of hardcoded IPs — instances come and go, and discovery keeps
the addresses current.
</Accordion>
<Accordion title="Distributed transactions & the saga pattern">
You can't do a two-phase commit across five databases in practice. When a business operation spans
services (place order → charge card → reserve stock), use a **saga**: a sequence of local
transactions, each publishing an event that triggers the next. If a step fails, run
**compensating** transactions to undo the earlier ones (refund the card, release the stock).
You give up atomicity for availability — the system is eventually consistent, not instantly
consistent.
</Accordion>
</Accordions>

## 🔨 Build this

<Steps>
<Step>
**Start with a monolith.** Take (or build) a small monolithic app — say an order system with
Users, Orders, and Notifications all in one codebase and one database. Get it working and note how
easy the in-process calls and single transaction are. This is your baseline.
</Step>
<Step>
**Extract one service with its own DB.** Pull *one* feature — Notifications is a good first cut —
into its own service with its **own database**. The monolith no longer reads that service's tables;
it calls the service's API instead. Congratulations: you now have a network boundary and two
deploys.
</Step>
<Step>
**Go async and add a gateway.** Replace the synchronous "send notification" call with an **event**:
the monolith publishes `OrderPlaced`, the notification service subscribes and reacts. Put an API
gateway in front so clients hit one URL, not two. Watch how the monolith no longer waits on (or
fails with) the notification service.
</Step>
<Step>
**Break it on purpose.** Kill the notification service mid-flow. Does an order still get placed?
Now introduce a saga: if payment fails after the order is created, emit a compensating event to
cancel the order. Trace one request across both services — feel how much harder observability just
got, and why you need it.
</Step>
</Steps>

## Interview signals & common pitfalls

- **Signal of mastery:** when asked "should we use microservices?", you push back with questions —
  team size, deploy pain, boundary clarity — and you're comfortable recommending "monolith first"
  when the split isn't justified.
- **Pitfall:** the shared-database anti-pattern — multiple services on the same tables. You've
  built a distributed monolith with all the cost and none of the independence.
- **Pitfall:** assuming microservices equal scalability. Scaling is a load-balancing and data
  problem; microservices are an *organizational* and *deployability* solution.
- **Pitfall:** chatty synchronous call chains — service A calls B calls C calls D — multiplying
  latency and coupling availability. Prefer async events for cross-service workflows.
- **Pitfall:** splitting too early, before you understand the domain. Boundaries are expensive to
  move once each service owns its data.

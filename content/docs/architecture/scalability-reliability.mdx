## Why this matters

Anyone can build a service that works when one user hits it on a good day. Senior engineers build
services that stay up when traffic 10x's, when a downstream dependency gets slow, and when a machine
quietly dies at 3am. **Scalability** is how you grow to handle more load; **reliability** is how you
keep working when parts of the system fail — and in a distributed system, something is always
failing. These are the skills that separate "it runs" from "it runs in production with real money on
the line." Every pattern here — retries, circuit breakers, backpressure, SLOs — exists because
someone learned the hard way what happens without it.

<MentorNote>
Don't confuse scalability with performance. **Performance** is how fast a single request is;
**scalability** is what happens to that speed as load grows. A blazing-fast service that falls over
at 100 requests per second is not scalable. And don't confuse *availability* with *reliability*: a
system can be available (responding) while returning wrong or degraded results. You want both.
</MentorNote>

## Mental model

Think of a busy restaurant, not a single heroic chef:

- **Vertical scaling** is hiring one superhuman chef with a bigger stove — powerful, simple, but
  there's a ceiling and if that one chef gets sick, dinner is cancelled.
- **Horizontal scaling** is hiring more line cooks — cheaper per unit, near-limitless, and if one
  calls in sick, service continues. This only works if any cook can handle any order (statelessness).
- **Redundancy** is keeping spare cooks and a backup stove. **N+1** means always having one more than
  you strictly need, so a failure doesn't drop you below capacity.
- **A single point of failure** is the one dishwasher everyone depends on — when it breaks, the whole
  kitchen stops. Find these and eliminate them.

The core insight: reliability comes from designing for failure, not from hoping it won't happen.
Assume every dependency will be slow, drop, or lie — then decide what your service does anyway.

## Core concepts to master

<Accordions type="single">
<Accordion title="Vertical vs horizontal scaling & statelessness">
**Vertical** (scale up) means a bigger machine — more CPU, RAM. Easy, but has a hard ceiling and a
single point of failure. **Horizontal** (scale out) means more machines behind a load balancer —
effectively unlimited, and it survives node loss. The catch: horizontal scaling only works if your
service is **stateless**. Push session state, in-flight data, and sticky context out to a shared
store (Redis, a database) so any instance can serve any request. Stateless services are the
foundation everything else in this page depends on.
</Accordion>
<Accordion title="Availability math: the nines">
Availability is uptime expressed in "nines." **99.9%** ("three nines") allows ~8.7 hours of downtime
a year; **99.99%** allows ~52 minutes; **99.999%** allows ~5 minutes. Two lessons: (1) each extra
nine costs roughly 10x more effort, so pick a target that matches the business, not vanity. (2)
Availability of a chain **multiplies** — five dependencies at 99.9% each give you only ~99.5%
combined. Redundancy fights this: two independent 99% components in parallel give ~99.99%.
</Accordion>
<Accordion title="Reliability patterns: timeouts, retries, backoff, circuit breakers">
The essential toolkit for talking to a flaky dependency. Always set **timeouts** — a request with no
timeout can hang forever and exhaust your threads. **Retry** transient failures, but with
**exponential backoff plus jitter** so you don't stampede a recovering service. Cap retries and only
retry **idempotent** operations. A **circuit breaker** (Fowler's pattern) watches the failure rate
and, once it trips, fails fast for a cooldown instead of hammering a dead dependency — then probes to
see if it recovered.
</Accordion>
<Accordion title="Bulkheads, backpressure, load shedding & graceful degradation">
**Bulkheads** isolate resources (separate thread pools or connection pools per dependency) so one
slow downstream can't drown the whole ship. **Backpressure** signals upstream to slow down when
you're saturated instead of silently queueing until you OOM. **Load shedding** deliberately rejects
low-priority work under overload to protect the core. **Graceful degradation** means serving a
reduced experience (cached data, no recommendations) rather than an error page — the site stays up
even when a feature is down.
</Accordion>
<Accordion title="Idempotency, failover & replication">
**Idempotency** means doing an operation twice has the same effect as once — essential because
retries and at-least-once delivery guarantee duplicates. Use idempotency keys for writes like
payments. **Replication** keeps copies of data (or a service) so a failure doesn't lose it;
**failover** is the automatic switch to a healthy replica when the primary dies. Understand the
tradeoffs: synchronous replication is safe but slow, asynchronous is fast but can lose recent writes.
</Accordion>
<Accordion title="SLIs, SLOs, SLAs & error budgets">
An **SLI** is a measured indicator (e.g. p99 latency, success rate). An **SLO** is your internal
target for that SLI (e.g. 99.9% of requests under 300ms). An **SLA** is the contractual promise to
customers, usually looser than the SLO, with penalties. The magic is the **error budget**: if your
SLO is 99.9%, you have 0.1% "budget" to spend on risk. Budget left? Ship features. Budget blown?
Freeze and fix reliability. This is how Google's SRE practice turns reliability into a number teams
can reason about.
</Accordion>
</Accordions>

## 🔨 Build this

<Steps>
<Step>
**Stand up a flaky dependency.** Write a tiny "downstream" service with a `/data` endpoint that
randomly fails (500s) 30% of the time and randomly adds a 2–5s delay. This is your unreliable world.
Put a second "frontend" service in front of it that just calls `/data` and returns the result — no
protection yet. Load-test it and watch failures and latency spikes pass straight through.
</Step>
<Step>
**Add timeouts, retries with backoff, and a cache.** Give the frontend a request **timeout** (say
1s). Retry transient failures with **exponential backoff plus jitter**, capped at 3 attempts. Add a
short-lived **cache** so repeated reads survive a downstream blip. Re-run the load test — success
rate should climb and tail latency should tighten.
</Step>
<Step>
**Wrap it in a circuit breaker and load-balance.** Add a **circuit breaker** around the downstream
call so that when failures cross a threshold, the frontend fails fast (or serves cached/degraded
data) instead of piling on. Run two or three instances of the downstream behind a **load balancer**
so a single dead instance is routed around.
</Step>
<Step>
**Inject failures and measure.** Deliberately kill one downstream instance, then crank the failure
rate to 100% and confirm the breaker trips and your frontend degrades gracefully instead of falling
over. Define an **SLO** (e.g. 99% success, p99 under 500ms), measure your SLIs during the chaos, and
write down how much error budget the experiment burned.
</Step>
</Steps>

## Cheat sheet

```text
Scaling        vertical = bigger box (ceiling + SPOF); horizontal = more boxes (needs stateless)
Availability   99.9% ≈ 8.7h/yr down · 99.99% ≈ 52m · 99.999% ≈ 5m · chained nines multiply
Timeout        every network call gets one — no unbounded waits
Retry          only idempotent ops · exponential backoff + jitter · cap attempts
Circuit break  trip on failure rate → fail fast for a cooldown → probe to recover
Bulkhead       isolate pools so one slow dep can't sink the whole service
Backpressure   signal upstream to slow down instead of queueing to OOM
Load shed      drop low-priority work under overload to protect the core
SLI/SLO/SLA    measured / internal target / customer contract · error budget = 1 - SLO
```

## Interview signals & common pitfalls

- **Signal of mastery:** you reach for "make it stateless and scale horizontally" instinctively, and
  you can walk through what happens to a request when a dependency goes slow — timeout, retry with
  backoff, circuit breaker, degrade — without being prompted.
- **Pitfall:** retrying without backoff or jitter, turning a small blip into a **retry storm** that
  keeps the recovering dependency down.
- **Pitfall:** retrying non-idempotent operations, so a "failed" payment actually charged twice.
- **Pitfall:** no timeouts — one hung downstream call exhausts your thread pool and takes the whole
  service with it.
- **Pitfall:** chasing five nines everywhere. Each nine costs ~10x; pick SLOs that match the business
  and spend the error budget on shipping.

Most people fail self-study not because the material is too hard, but because their *method* is
weak. Here's how to get the most out of this program.

## The anatomy of every topic page

Every one of the 17 topic pages follows the same shape, so you always know where to look:

<Steps>
<Step>**Why it matters** — the one-paragraph reason a senior cares about this.</Step>
<Step>**Mental model** — a plain-English analogy so the concepts have somewhere to hang.</Step>
<Step>**Core concepts** — the things you must be able to explain, no fluff.</Step>
<Step>**Watch** — a curated, verified set of videos, playable inline.</Step>
<Step>**Read** — the best free docs, articles and books.</Step>
<Step>**Build this** — the lab. This is the part that creates real skill.</Step>
<Step>**Interview signals & pitfalls** — how mastery is judged, and where people faceplant.</Step>
<Step>**Mastery checklist** — tick items off; your progress is saved in your browser.</Step>
</Steps>

## Five rules that make it stick

<MentorNote>
These aren't productivity fluff — they're the difference between *remembering this in a year* and
*forgetting it by Friday*.
</MentorNote>

1. **Build before you feel ready.** You learn the shape of a problem by hitting its edges, not by
   watching someone else avoid them. Start the lab while you're still a little confused.
2. **Explain it out loud.** If you can't teach a concept to a rubber duck in three sentences, you
   don't understand it yet. Go back.
3. **Space your repetition.** Revisit last week's checklist before starting a new topic. Five minutes
   of recall beats an hour of re-reading.
4. **Break things on purpose.** Kill a container. Pull a network cable. Send a malformed message.
   Seniors are calm in failure because they've *seen* failure — in safe places, first.
5. **Keep a "learnings" log.** One paragraph per day: what you built, what broke, what you'd do
   differently. In 90 days it becomes proof of how far you've come.

## What "mastery" means here

You've mastered a topic when you can do all three:

<Tabs items={["Explain", "Build", "Debug"]}>
<Tab value="Explain">
You can describe the concept, its trade-offs, and *when not to use it* — without notes. If someone
asks "why not just X?", you have a real answer.
</Tab>
<Tab value="Build">
You've built something non-trivial with it yourself, from an empty folder, and it works. You know
which commands and config actually matter.
</Tab>
<Tab value="Debug">
When it breaks, you know where to look first. You can read the logs/metrics and form a hypothesis
instead of randomly changing things.
</Tab>
</Tabs>

## A note on the videos

Every video linked in this guide was **verified to exist** — the links point to real, well-known
creators (Fireship, ByteByteGo, TechWorld with Nana, Hussein Nasser, freeCodeCamp and more). Watch
the short "in 100 seconds"-style clips for the mental model, and pick **one** longer course per topic
— you don't need to watch all of them. Depth over breadth.
